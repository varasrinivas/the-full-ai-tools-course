  GROUND TRUTH, ApprovalService.java line 99:
        if (score > AUTO_APPROVE_THRESHOLD) {       <-- STRICT, not inclusive

  *** THE SMALL MODEL WAS WRONG — AND WRONG IN THE WORST WAY. ***
  It looked in the right places (javadoc, constant, decision block) but
  reported the JAVADOC'S SEMANTICS as the CODE'S BEHAVIOUR, and flagged no
  discrepancy at all. The answer is fluent, structured, and carries precise
  line-number citations, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: a developer
  implementing from it would believe they were matching production. It cost
  $0.0368 and it would have shipped a false belief about the system.
  The large model's entire contribution on this task was NOTICING THE CONFLICT
  — which is the part that cannot be bought cheaply.

=== THE RULE, NOW DEMONSTRATED RATHER THAN ASSERTED ===
  BIG MODEL TO PLAN, SMALL MODEL TO GRIND.
   - Grind = mechanical, verifiable, one right answer you can check (counts,
     transformations, format conversions, boilerplate). Small model: same
     answer, cheaper. Use it.
   - Judgment = the value is in noticing something (a contradiction, a risk, an
     absence, a scope decision). Small model: confident, cheap, and it may miss
     the very thing you were paying for — with citations that look authoritative.
  The tell is not task SIZE, it is whether you can CHECK the answer. A grind
  answer you can verify in seconds; a judgment answer you cannot verify without
  redoing the judgment, which is why you bought the model in the first place.
  Ties to M-8.3: "confidently wrong and cheap is worse than expensive and right."

=== FALLBACK CHAINS ===
  The sane pattern is not "pick one model", it is: small model first for work
  that is checkable, escalate on failure or on any task whose output you cannot
  verify. Escalation must be triggered by the TASK TYPE, not by the model's
  confidence — the small model was maximally confident while being wrong.

=== THREE-WAY COST-MODEL COMPARISON (structure, not price points) ===
  Prices change; the STRUCTURES are stable and are what actually shape behaviour:
   - Claude Code: metered/usage-visible. Every turn has a number attached — which
     is precisely why this entire track could be measured at all. Waste is visible,
     so there is a feedback loop that teaches efficiency.
   - GitHub Copilot: per-seat subscription. Cost is predictable and decoupled from
     usage — no per-action feedback, so nothing in the pricing teaches restraint;
     budgeting is a headcount question, not an engineering one.
   - Cursor: per-seat with usage-based components — a hybrid; predictable floor,
     variable ceiling once heavier agent use kicks in.
  DO NOT quote current per-seat or per-token prices from this course; check the
  vendors. What generalises: metered pricing makes waste legible (and is why
  M-8.2's 9.7x paste penalty was discoverable), seat pricing hides it.
